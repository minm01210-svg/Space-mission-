REFERENCE SYSTEM

The equations behind a spacecraft mission.

A practical checklist of the maths, physical inputs, and engineering specifications needed to model launch and interplanetary travel.

Launch and propulsion

From thrust on the pad to injected mass

Thrust-to-weight ratio

Check whether the vehicle can lift away from the launch pad.

T/W = T / (m · g₀)T = thrust · m = current vehicle mass · g₀ = 9.80665 m/s²
For liftoff, T/W must be greater than 1.0; real launch vehicles use margin.

Tsiolkovsky rocket equation

The primary ideal velocity equation for a burn or stage.

Δv = Isp · g₀ · ln(m₀ / m₁)m₀ = initial mass · m₁ = final mass · Isp = specific impulse

Propellant mass

Invert the rocket equation to size the propellant load.

mₚ = m₁ · (e^(Δv/(Isp·g₀)) − 1)mₚ = propellant mass · m₁ includes dry mass and payload

Mass flow rate

Estimate how quickly propellant is consumed during a burn.

ṁ = T / (Isp · g₀)ṁ = mass flow rate in kg/s. Use actual throttle and mixture data for detailed work.

Ideal launch budget

A rough ascent budget separates orbital speed from real losses.

Δvₗₐᵤₙcₕ ≈ Δvₒᵣᵦᵢₜ + gravity loss + drag loss + steering lossFor LEO, orbital speed is about 7.67 km/s; the complete ascent budget is higher.

Staging

Each stage contributes its own ideal Δv before separation.

Δvₜₒₜₐₗ = Σ [Ispᵢ · g₀ · ln(m₀ᵢ / m₁ᵢ)]Include tanks, engines, residuals, payload, and separation hardware in each stage mass.

Orbit and interplanetary travel

Reference equations for transfer design

Circular orbital velocity

v꜀ = √(μ / r)μ = GM of the central body · r = distance from its centre

Escape velocity

vₑₛ꜀ = √(2μ / r)For a body with atmosphere, this is not the same as a practical ascent requirement.

Vis-viva equation

v² = μ · (2/r − 1/a)a = semi-major axis of the spacecraft orbit

Hohmann transfer

aₜ = (r₁+r₂)/2
Δv₁ = √(μ/r₁) · (√(2r₂/(r₁+r₂)) − 1)
Δv₂ = √(μ/r₂) · (1 − √(2r₁/(r₁+r₂)))Assumes circular, coplanar orbits and impulsive burns.

Transfer time

t = π · √(aₜ³ / μ)For a half-ellipse Hohmann transfer. Real missions may use higher-energy, lower-energy, or gravity-assist trajectories.

Hyperbolic excess and C3

v∞ = √(v² − vₑₛ꜀²)
C3 = v∞²C3 is the characteristic energy used to describe departure from a planetary sphere of influence.

Plane change

Δv = 2v · sin(Δi / 2)Perform plane changes where orbital velocity is low whenever possible.

Patched-conic approximation

Δvₜₒₜₐₗ ≈ departure + heliocentric transfer + arrival/captureUseful for preliminary design; replace with ephemerides and numerical propagation for final analysis.

Communication delay

tₗᵢₙₖ = d / cd = spacecraft distance · c = 299,792,458 m/s. Mars one-way delay varies with planetary geometry.

Atmospheric entry and landing

Use these with a real atmosphere and aerodynamic model

Exponential density model

ρ(h) = ρ₀ · e^(−h/H)ρ₀ = reference density · H = scale height · h = altitude. This is a rough model, not a weather forecast.

Atmospheric drag

D = ½ · ρ · v² · Cᴅ · ACd = drag coefficient · A = reference area · v must be relative to the atmosphere.

Ballistic coefficient

β = m / (Cᴅ · A)High β vehicles decelerate deeper in an atmosphere; low β vehicles experience more gradual deceleration.

Dynamic pressure

q = ½ · ρ · v²Peak dynamic pressure, or Max Q, is a major structural and aerodynamic design constraint.

Lift and lift-to-drag ratio

L = ½ · ρ · v² · Cᴸ · A
L/D = Cᴸ / CᴅBank angle, attitude, and changing coefficients determine how much the vehicle can steer.

Powered descent

T − mg − D = m · aFor landing, add engine throttle limits, propellant reserves, terrain height, guidance, and touchdown velocity constraints.

Mars-specific minimum inputs: pressure and density versus altitude, seasonal temperature, winds, vehicle area, Cd/Cl tables, heat-shield limits, parachute data, engine thrust curve, landing-site elevation, and terrain slope.

Minimum data needed to turn equations into a simulation

SystemRequired specificationWhy it matters
Spacecraft massDry mass, payload mass, propellant mass, residuals, stage massesDetermines mass ratio, Δv, acceleration, and propellant reserve.
PropulsionThrust, vacuum/sea-level Isp, throttle range, burn time, mixture ratio, mass flowDetermines Δv, burn duration, acceleration, and staging.
GeometryReference area, Cd, Cl, dimensions, attitude limits, heat-shield areaNeeded for drag, lift, heating, and entry dynamics.
Launch vehicleStage inert mass, propellant capacity, thrust curve, Isp curve, fairing volume, payload limitDetermines whether the spacecraft can reach the required injection orbit.
DestinationGM, radius, atmosphere, rotation, orbital elements, target orbit or landing altitudeDefines transfer energy, capture, entry, and surface conditions.
TrajectoryEpoch, state vector or orbital elements, launch azimuth, burn timing, guidance modelTurns a reference path into a time-dependent trajectory.

LandingParachute size, heat shield, lift-to-drag, powered-descent thrust, terrain, touchdown limitsNeeded to decide whether “reaching Mars” also means landing safely.
